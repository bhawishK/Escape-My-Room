## Sand Table: Commanding a Shared Real Home Across Scales and Devices

[Authors, affiliations]

### Abstract

We present *Sand Table*, a 3D user interface for shared spatial interaction between a person in a VR headset and a person holding a phone, centred on a real place: the phone user's own home.

- **The occupant** films their home once with a phone, and it becomes a photoreal Gaussian splat.
- **The commander** holds that home as a live dollhouse in a Quest headset. From it, they choose a real object and set it on fire, guide the occupant with a giant hand and a path drawn on their floor, and watch where the occupant is and which way they face.
- **The occupant** sees the same fire, generated by AI from the object's own appearance, at full size over their real room in the phone's browser, together with the hand and the path.

We contribute five interaction techniques that work across scales and devices, and report the design iterations that shaped them.

### 1 Introduction

Most people never practise escaping their own home, although fire-safety guidance asks them to. Familiarity with exits strongly shapes escape choices [Kinateder et al.]. VR fire training, meanwhile, takes place in generic or purpose-modelled buildings [reviews].

We ask what it takes for two people on different devices to rehearse an emergency together in the occupant's *real* home:
- one inside it, at true scale, with only a phone;
- one remote, with a headset and an overview.

This is the contest's problem in a concrete form: different viewpoints, inputs and levels of immersion over one shared space.

### 2 System overview

[Figure 1: left, the commander's dollhouse of a bedroom on the Quest, with the occupant's figure and view cone, and the bed burning; right, the phone at 1:1 over the real bed, with the giant hand and the amber path to the door.]

1. **Capture.** A coach on the phone guides one video of the room. A server reconstructs it as a 3D Gaussian splat, lays out its objects as boxes (SpatialLM), and builds a positioning map.
2. **Shared session.** Both devices join one session in the browser (WebXR on Quest 3; WebXR with tracking on the phone). The server keeps one room state, one physics simulation (Havok) and one clock.
3. **Hazards.** For a chosen object, the server cuts its picture from the room's own splat, asks an image-to-video model (Wan 2.2) for the object catching fire, checks the video automatically, and lifts it to animated 3D Gaussians (L4GM). Each fire is placed in the object's box and cached for that room.

### 3 Interaction techniques

**T1, a personal sand table (extended).** The commander's view of the home is a world-in-miniature [Stoakley et al.] built from the occupant's own capture and kept live:
- two-handed pinch shrinks or grows it;
- one hand moves and turns it;
- everything the occupant does appears in it.

Unlike a dollhouse of a designed space [Dollhouse VR] or a miniature cut from a live capture [VirtualNexus], it is the occupant's whole home, linked to a person standing in it.

**T2, commanding a drill from the miniature (created).** From a wrist menu, the commander:
- chooses one of the room's real objects;
- prepares its hazard, then starts and stops it.

The fire replaces the object at the same moment in the miniature and in the occupant's real room, on one shared clock. The occupant is told only that a drill is coming until it starts. We found no AR–VR system in which the remote person both triggers events in the trainee's real space and guides them.

**T3, a giant hand with a path (adapted).** The commander's hand inside the miniature appears to the occupant as a giant hand reaching into their room [Stafford et al.; Piumsomboon et al.]. When the commander points with a finger, the spot is drawn on the occupant's floor as a ring, with a path from where they stand. When viewpoints differ, a hand alone is hard to read, so we pair it with a pointer [Kim et al.].

**T4, occupant awareness (adapted).** The occupant appears in the miniature as a figure with a view cone, so the commander sees where they are and which way they face [On the Shoulder of the Giant].

**T5, cross-scale shared manipulation (extended).**
- Both people move the same real-room objects.
- The phone carries them at true scale by aiming at the floor.
- The headset carries them in the miniature or at life size, and can throw them.
- One server-side physics simulation keeps both views in agreement [ShareVR; Grandi et al.].

### 4 Implementation

- **Pages and rendering:** both run in the browser, so there is nothing to install:
  - the phone page in three.js with Spark for splats;
  - the headset page in the Immersive Web SDK.
  - Hazard frames are compressed to SPZ (≈176 KB each), and low-end phones load every other frame.
- **Server:** a FastAPI server holds the session, physics and clock.
- **GPU work:** room reconstruction and the 3D step of hazards run on one desktop GPU (RTX 3070 Ti, 8 GB).
- **Hosted models:** pictures and videos come from hosted models.
- **Fire quality checks,** automatic at each stage:
  - objects the capture barely saw are refused;
  - videos whose camera moves are refused, and the best of two is kept;
  - the 3D result is checked against its video.
- **Session logs** record each drill run (positions, guidance, hazard events) for evaluation.

### 5 Design process and iterations

- **Choosing objects by touch → a menu.** Touching an object in the miniature to choose it read as unclear in use on the Quest. A menu mirroring the page's Drill card replaced it.
- **The Quest's floor estimate was off,** so objects floated. We added touching the real floor with a flat palm to set it.
- **Head-gesture yes/no triggered while looking around.** We required quick swings about one direction.
- **Hazard pictures from the splat were unreadable to the video model** when taken square-on and low; it invented another object. Picturing from a corner, 35° up, fixed this.

### 6 Evaluation [optional; to fill if the pilot runs]

[Pilot, n = 8–12.]
- **Part A, participant as occupant** with a scripted commander. Guidance conditions: voice only; hand + voice; hand + path + voice.
- **Part B, participant as commander.**
- **Measures:**
  - from the session logs: time to the first step and to the exit, path length against the shortest, and guided share;
  - questionnaires: NASA-TLX, co-presence items, and ease and fun.
- **Safety:** a DRILL label, no alarm sounds, a cleared path with a spotter, and consent for home scans.

### 7 Limitations

- Generated fire *looks* plausible but is not physically simulated. Its timing is not validated against measured burns.
- Reconstructed objects come out thicker than real ones.
- Live hazard generation takes minutes and depends on hosted GPU quotas, so a demo uses fires made in advance.
- Evaluated in one home so far.

### 8 Conclusion

Sand Table shows that a commander and an occupant on different devices can share one real home at two scales:
- each acting on it in the way their device suits;
- the commander changing what happens in it;
- the occupant experiencing that at true scale.
